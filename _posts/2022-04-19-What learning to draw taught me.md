In a cold season some time ago I was looking around for something do in my evenings and I happened across a life drawing class in my local gallery. I was bored and I was arrogant, thinking that draftsmanship was another string in my bow. I told myself that I was going along out of curiosity, to learn a new skill, to participate in some historic ritual. I would be dispassionate, an objective recording machine, a camera. I was lying to myself, and revealing in my own ignorance. Naturally I went along to look at naked people for a couple of hours. And needless to say: I wasn’t getting laid at that time.

Art has a habit of showing you who you are, like Frankenstein your creations take on a new life separate but connected to you. I can see now that I was afraid of my emotions, burying them so deeply I couldn’t say I felt anything except an anaesthetic pleasantness. Often what I felt was neutral, orthogonal to the line of pleasure and pain. Drawing started as an attempt at mastery, but ended in self-love.

My plan was only hampered by the fact that I hadn’t attended art class since I was thirteen. Which was literally half a lifetime ago. I was undeterred, assuming that mastery was only a matter of applying myself diligently, part-time, and on top of all the other things I had going in my life already.

High walls, cool air. Natural light falling from tall windows. In the corner, a CD player whispering bossa nova. The odour of turps. Easels arranged in a semi-circle, artists setting up. From the closet comes the model. She walks to the locus of the circle, disrobes and strikes the first pose.

I stood at twenty past the hour, and saw her as the last of the summer evening sun undulated over her body.  So, I did the only thing I knew how to, which was to follow the outline of the body. I traced her shape onto my page with the tip of a charcoal vine, which slid l like velvet ink over my paper. There wasn’t time to look at the drawing. The model struck an athletic, energised, forceful pose. I couldn’t look away.

And then the minute was up. Surely, the drawing would look good?

The instructor thought differently.

“In relation to the thigh where is the bottom of the hand?” She asked me.

“In the middle of the thigh.”

“Look again, please,” she continued.

And then I saw it. I had drawn the arms comically short, finishing well above the waist, when the model’s arms all well into the thigh. Also I had depicted the model as far more unctuous than she really was. She looked more like the love child  of blancmange and a t-rex.

Having such direct data that your perceptions are way off is a queer feeling. It was like a dream where you were falling into endless dark and you awake, and you feel a sense of vertigo yet your feet feel directly next your head. I could feel my brain reconfiguring itself, like molten plastic, to make sense of everything I was learning.

Drawing plunged me into a sort of existential crisis. If my perceptions of the world were wrong, how could I trust my perceptions of myself? After all, I couldn’t even see where I’d gone wrong. But I had enjoyed the feeling of making the picture so much that I didn’t care that my drawings weren’t even garbage.

It turns out that diligent practice at something does, in fact, make you better at it. And the good thing about being bad at something is that you get better at it very quickly. This is extremely motivating. Over the course of a couple of years my drawings took on accurate proportions and gained the gesture of the pose. My drawings actually looked like models in the pose, instead of vaguely misshapen blobs.

Still, something was missing. However much I empathised with the model, and however much I followed the process, my drawings lacked something that other artists were able to capture. Their drawings were littered with faults, but had energy and dynamism. Life.

That was until a new model started in the rotation. Let’s call him David.

“Oh that’s good,” my teacher said, evening up my first drawing of David. “You’ve really captured something of him.”

Drawing David was an experience in total absorption. Where I put aside any pretence of creating a ‘good’ or a ‘bad’ drawing. In doing so, I’d unwittingly committed something real to the page. So much so, that I didn’t notice it in the moment. The only recored of that enjoyment was the drawing.

David was muscular and hung. David was a Daddy. And I had enjoyed drawing him immensely.

There was something else at work too. Growing up, guys like me were never sexy. I never had a lithe swimmer’s body that would grace the cover of GQ. (And I certainly don’t have it now!) Think Andrew Garfield in Spiderman. This was in the early 00s, when muscle wasn’t in. By the time I was 25 I though the window for ‘being sexy’ was over. Such was my immaturity. David offered a different model for me: here was someone I could be. Someone that was still sexy. My drawing of him contained some hope, as well as a certain regard.

I wasn’t getting laid because I was ashamed of myself, and I carried that shame around with me like a ball and chain. At one point I was almost convinced that you could tell how ashamed I was through my breathing in a darkened room. I was ashamed about how chunky I was, how I couldn’t afford clothes that fit, and how I walked around with this grey cloud of shame. Since I graduated university I’d carried around an apron of fat in front of my torso, which I mostly detested but refused to actually do anything to get rid of. I had gotten a lot more attention from boys when I was slimmer, and that attention both thrilled and terrified me. Drawing brought these judgements home. When you’re in a chilly art studio staring at someone naked all your judgements about the model get reflected back on yourself. And then you think: well if they can be so brave, why can’t I?

I’d gotten past 25 without ever having a serious, committed relationship. It’s probably doesn’t matter in the large scheme of things, but I was ashamed of this as well. I’d not had one partly because I didn’t know what I really wanted, and because I’d not gone out and found it. I didn’t have much in common with guys my age because, so guys who would go for dinner found me boring. We had nothing to talk about.

This was shame working.

David, however, was sexy. And seeing a guy I could be standing in front of, month after month, gave me a little confidence. Somebody out there would find me sexy. So I put ‘looking for life models’ in my Grindr profile.

And would you know, a few months later a cute guy messaged me.
